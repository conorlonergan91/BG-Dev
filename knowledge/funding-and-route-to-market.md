# Funding & Route to Market

How a game actually gets *released* — and, for this studio, how to do it with **minimal money out of the
designer's pocket and minimal financial risk** (a first-class studio constraint; see
`designer-profile.md`). This is a *strategy* decision that comes **before** campaign copy or a sell sheet:
which route, given the goal and the capital you're willing to risk. Owned by **`bg-funding`**;
`bg-marketing` then produces the materials for the chosen route, and `bg-publishing` handles its IP/
compliance/contracts. *(All figures 2025–2026 and move fast — re-verify live via `bg-research`.)*

## The one-line map (cheapest → most expensive, upfront, to *you*)
**Free/portfolio (≈€0) → pitch/licence to a publisher (low hundreds) → POD (tens–low hundreds) → hybrid/
staged → self-run crowdfunding (thousands–tens of thousands).** The 2026 tariff/shipping environment makes
anything *you* manufacture-and-ship riskier and pushes the risk-averse designer toward **licensing** and
**POD**.

## The routes

### 1. Free / portfolio release — ≈€0, builds what money can't
Release a **free print-and-play or a public Screentop.gg game** (the studio's own prototyping platform —
zero extra tooling). Cost ~€0; risk none. The payoff isn't money — it's the two assets every *later* route
needs: **proof-of-fun and an audience/mailing list.** For a newer designer this is the highest-leverage
cheap move and should almost always come first.

### 2. Pitch / licence to a publisher — lowest upfront, lowest risk of any *distributed* product
Sign the game to a publisher who **fronts ALL costs** (art, development, manufacturing, marketing,
distribution, and all tariff/inventory risk) for a **royalty**. Structurally the cheapest, least risky
route — **the default recommendation for a risk-averse hobbyist whose goal is "get a real game out there."**
- **Upfront cost:** just what it takes to *pitch* — a **one-page sell sheet**, a **working print-and-play /
  Screentop prototype**, maybe a short overview video and travel. **Publishers do NOT want finished art**
  (they redo it), so you spend nothing on illustration. This plays to the studio's strength: a tight,
  well-tested prototype.
- **Royalty reality:** typically **~5–8% of *wholesale/net*** (not MSRP). Wholesale ≈ 40% of MSRP, so "5%"
  ≈ **~2.4% of MSRP**. New designers usually land **~5%**. **Advances** exist in only about half of
  contracts, mostly **$1–2k**, and a bigger advance usually trades against a lower rate. Paid quarterly.
- **Trade-off:** you give up **control and most of the upside** for zero financial risk.
- **How signing actually happens:** convention **pitch appointments** (book **6–8 weeks ahead** — slots
  fill fast; ~10–15 min: explain, play a sample round, Q&A), **Protospiel**-type events, and *targeted*
  cold submissions (check they're open, personalise, lead with the sell sheet + video, follow their form).
  **Match the publisher's tier** — many only take **small-box games under $20**, which is exactly this
  studio's lane.

### 3. Print-on-demand (POD) — near-zero upfront, thin margin
Platforms print/ship each copy on order — no inventory, no minimum. *Storefront POD* (The Game Crafter
sells to the public, pays you a cut) or *at-cost POD* (DriveThruCards, MakePlayingCards — you/buyers order
at cost).
- **Upfront:** essentially proof copies (~€15–60 for a card game) + your design time.
- **Risk:** near-zero — the buyer's payment covers the print.
- **Margin:** the catch. Per-unit costs are high (printing ones and twos), so on a storefront the designer
  nets only **a few dollars per copy**; at-cost card printing runs from **~$8–$26 for a single deck** down
  to **~$8/deck** at POD bulk tiers. **A proving ground and long-tail trickle, not a business.**
- **Right when:** you want a *real, buyable* copy this month with zero risk, for a **small, card-forward,
  few-component-type** game (POD does cards/tiles well; custom minis/boxes poorly and expensively).

### 4. Self-run crowdfunding — "pre-funded" manufacturing, but not free, and highest risk
Backers pay **before** you pay the factory, so the *print run* is pre-funded — the real appeal. **But you
still front everything that makes the campaign look fundable, and that number has climbed sharply.**
- **Upfront the designer still pays:** art/illustration (often the biggest line), graphic design + page,
  presentation prototypes (~$150–200 each), a video, and — the big one — **paid ads / pre-launch lead-gen
  at roughly 15–25% of the funding goal** (≈$15–25k to raise $100k). Platform fee ~5% + processing ~3–5% +
  pledge-manager processing again. **Indirect costs commonly total 23–38% of gross pledges; ~10–30% is
  marketing alone.** First-timers often pay **$4–12k** for guided support.
- **Risk:** that pre-launch spend is **sunk whether or not you fund**, and even funded campaigns can be
  wrecked post-campaign by **tariff/freight spikes** on fulfilment (pricing set before a cost jump).
- **Upside:** highest of any route *if it works* — you keep the publisher's margin, not a royalty.
- **Market reality:** softening — KS tabletop dollars have fallen several years running (avg KS tabletop
  campaign ~$41k); commentators call 2025 "the end of the golden age" for overproduced crowdfunding.
- **Only after** you've built an audience/list, the game has shelf appeal, and you'll run a marketing
  operation. **Minimise it:** use a **manufacture-and-fulfil partner** (no self-warehousing), fulfil to
  **regional EU/UK hubs**, keep art scope tight, and come in with a **warm list** (kills the biggest cost
  line). See `manufacturing-and-fulfillment.md`.

### 5. Hybrid / staged — the risk-minimiser's actual playbook
Don't pick one; **sequence** so each stage is paid for by traction, not your wallet:
1. **Prove it free** (Screentop / P&P) → plays, feedback, testimonials, email list.
2. **Optionally POD it** → a buyable copy for reviewers/superfans + a small trickle, still zero inventory
   risk.
3. **Then branch by goal:** want a released game with **no risk** → **pitch** (now backed by proof-of-fun
   and an audience); want **upside/control** and you've built a list → **crowdfund** with a warm audience.

## Design decisions that keep *every* route cheaper
A **small-box, low-component, few-component-*type*** game is cheaper to POD, cheaper to crowdfund-fulfil,
and easier to pitch (fits sub-$20 slots). Concretely: **minimise unique component types**, use **standard
63×88 mm cards**, keep the **box small and light** (freight + tariff exposure), use **double-sided/multi-use**
components, and **keep text minimal** (lower reading-level *and* cheaper to localise). **This is already the
elegance house style** — so the studio's aesthetic *is* its cheapest-to-release strategy. Cross-ref
`cost-and-manufacturability.md`.

## The recommendation framework (for minimal upfront cost)
1. **Always start free** (Screentop/P&P) — €0, produces proof-of-fun + a list.
2. **Default to pitching/licensing** if the goal is a real, distributed game with no financial risk — trade
   control/upside for the publisher fronting 100% of cost and all tariff risk. Best odds with a small-box
   sub-$20 design (this studio's lane).
3. **POD in parallel** for a buyable copy + trickle; a proving ground, not income.
4. **Stage it** — free → (POD) → pitch *or* crowdfund once demand is proven. The actual recommended path.
5. **Self-crowdfund last**, only with a warm list and an appetite for the upside, control, marketing work,
   and tariff-exposed fulfilment.
